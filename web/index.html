It works like an exam hall

You already know the rules that make a school exam fair. Proctor applies the same four to AI.

  1. The questions stay sealed

    An expert writes questions with one correct answer and locks them. A fingerprint of the set goes on the public record first, so the questions can't be swapped later.

  2. The exam is sat behind a locked door

    The questions are opened only inside secure hardware, where the AI answers them. Nobody outside can look in, not the AI's maker and not Proctor.

  3. A machine marks it, with no opinions

    Each answer is checked against the answer key by a short program. There is no second AI judging the first, and no person who can be leaned on.

  4. The result goes on the noticeboard

    Every score is written to NEAR, a public record nobody can edit. The question writer is paid each time their questions are used.

A high score can mean the AI is capable. It can also mean it has read the answers.

How AI is tested today

  • The test questions are published online.
  • AI models learn from what is online, tests included.
  • Keeping a test private doesn't help: to use it you must send it to the AI's maker.
  • The company that built the AI often reports its own score.

With Proctor

  • Questions are sealed from the day they are written.
  • They are opened only inside hardware nobody can look into.
  • If an AI's maker could have read a set, that set is flagged for good.
  • Scores are recorded by a public contract, not reported by anyone.

Questions people ask

Who is this for?

Anyone who has to choose an AI and can't afford to be wrong: a hospital, a law firm, a bank, a school. It is also for experts who want to be paid for writing good questions in their field.

What does "sealed" mean?

The questions are encrypted so that only one exact version of the marking program can open them, and only inside secure hardware. The writer's own computer is the last place they exist in readable form.

Can Proctor read the questions or change a score?

No. We never hold the questions in readable form, and scores are stored by a public contract that checks each one came from the right marking program and the right question set.

What if the AI runs on its maker's own servers?

Then its maker could have read the questions, and Proctor says so. The set is permanently marked as seen by that company. Models hosted inside secure hardware don't have this problem.

What if a question or its answer is wrong?

Once a set is published, anyone can challenge an answer by putting up a small deposit. If the challenge holds, they are paid from the writer's stake and every score is recounted without that question.

Is this live?

It runs today on NEAR's test network with real AI models. Every number on this page is read from the contract when you open it. It has not had a security audit.
